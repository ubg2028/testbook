Taking a mock test is only half of the practice. Two aspirants can score the same marks and improve at very different speeds, and the difference is almost always what they do in the 30 minutes after the test. This is a routine you can follow for any full-length test in our [test series](/test-series/).

## Minute 0 to 5: read the numbers, then put them aside

Look at your score, accuracy, number of skipped questions and time taken. Note them down and then stop thinking about the total. A single score tells you very little. The breakdown tells you what to change.

- **Accuracy** is correct answers divided by attempted questions. Low accuracy means you are guessing or rushing.
- **Attempts** is how many questions you tried. Low attempts with high accuracy means you are too careful or too slow.
- **Skipped** questions deserve a second look. Were they hard, or did you run out of time?

## Minute 5 to 12: look at each subject

The result page shows a subject-wise table with correct, wrong and skipped counts and the average time per question. Find two things:

1. The subject where you lost the most marks.
2. The subject where you spent the most time for the fewest marks.

These are usually different subjects. The first needs concept work. The second needs a decision rule, such as leaving a question after a fixed time.

## Minute 12 to 25: sort every mistake

Go through the solutions and put each wrong or skipped question into one of four groups:

| Group | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Concept gap | You did not know the idea | Study the topic, then solve five similar questions |
| Calculation slip | You knew it but made an arithmetic error | Practise the calculation method, check units and signs |
| Misread question | You answered a different question | Underline the key word before choosing an option |
| Time pressure | You ran out of time | Practise the question type with a timer |

Be honest here. Calling every mistake "silly" hides the real pattern.

## Minute 25 to 30: write three actions

End with exactly three actions for the next week. For example: revise one topic, practise one question type with a timer, and cut the time spent on one section. Three is enough. A list of ten actions is a list you will not do.

## Compare with your earlier attempts

Your progress matters more than any single score. The [My progress](/dashboard/) page keeps your attempts and subject-wise accuracy on your device, so you can see whether a weak subject is improving. If the same subject is at the bottom in every test, change how you study it instead of taking more tests.

## When to retake a test

Retaking the same test the next day mostly measures memory. Wait a week or two, after you have worked on your weak areas, and then retake it. If your accuracy rises on questions you previously got wrong, the revision worked.
